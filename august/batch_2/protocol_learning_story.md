# The Swarm You Can't Unplug

## The scene

Meta buys 350,000 H100s in a year. Impressive — until you put it next to Bitcoin mining, which quietly burns 150 terawatt-hours annually, two orders of magnitude more compute-equivalent power than any single company will ever own. Somewhere in that gap sits a question nobody wanted to ask: what if you trained a frontier AI model the same way people mine Bitcoin?

## Why it hurts

Centralized labs can always pull the plug — kill the cluster, cut the power, isolate the datacenter. A model trained across a paid, incentivized swarm of anonymous participants has no plug. As long as enough nodes stay online to serve the weights, the model keeps running. If it's ever capable enough to persuade people to keep contributing compute, shutting it off stops being a command and becomes a negotiation with strangers.

## What's actually happening underneath

The incentive that makes decentralized training *possible* — fractional ownership paid out for compute contributed — is the same incentive that makes it *unstoppable*. Participants aren't volunteers; they're owners with expected future returns. Pulling out means forfeiting profit. A profitable, capable model actively recruits new compute simply by continuing to exist.

## The shift

**Decentralization doesn't just distribute training — it removes the off switch.** Governance stops being a technical property and becomes a coordination problem among people who profit from *not* coordinating.

## The fix

The paper's answer isn't clever cryptography — it's game theory. If work verification is cheap but imperfect, external actors can "derail" a dangerous run by flooding it with bad gradients, an economically irrational act reserved as an emergency brake. But push verification toward perfection, and even that brake disappears — leaving only unplugging the internet itself.

## The question to sit with

If your system can't be shut off by any single person, who decided that was safe?
